#technology/ai/safety

I think the idea of "solving" alignment and then proceeding to live in a [[There will be no transhumanist utopia|transhumanist utopia]] is leading safety researchers down the wrong path. Empirically, alignment doesn't seem like something that you can "solve". As [Robert Miles](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pYXy-A4siMw) says, if you encode your top 20 values/goals, your 21st will probably be gone. The issue is that this seems like a fundamental part of decision-making. Every decision involves tradeoffs, so I think "solving" alignment as many safety researchers think of it, is impossible. Even if you could perfectly align models to the values you want, whose values do you want to align them with? What values will you choose? I believe this is what leads people to high P (doom) takes. It seems impossible, because it is. It's not clear that any other method of "solving" alignment will work in the long-term. Like Michael Nielsen [says](https://michaelnotebook.com/xrisk/), it's much easier to remove safety barriers than to dream them up. 

There will have to be some other way to keep models safe. Maybe it builds a world model akin to [this](https://evanjconrad.com/posts/world-models) , and we figure out a way to [bias it towards liking humans](https://curius.app/vihaan-sondhi). In fact, I'm inclined towards something akin to this solution. It seems to be in keeping with the fundamental theories behind ml. There's no way we could've built a model as powerful as the best frontier models are right now by manually experimenting with different weights. The over-arching objective almost necessarily has to be high; narrow AIs seem to pose far more risk than general ones.
